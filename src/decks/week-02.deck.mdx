# Dating techniques

SLOP2374 · Week 2

---

## The log is a primary source

`git log` records what happened, when, and by whom.

It does not record why, what was argued about, or what was deleted before anyone
committed it.

Read it for what it is: an incomplete register kept by people who were not
writing it for you.

---

## Three phases

- **Construction:** high frequency, few authors, large diffs
- **Maintenance:** lower frequency, more authors, more merges
- **Decay:** sporadic, small, increasingly automated

Most projects pass through all three. The interesting part is the boundary.

---

## Boundary events

A week that does not look like the weeks around it.

- A spike: a release, a rewrite, a deadline
- A drop: a departure, a fork, a funding decision
- A gap that never closes

The boundary is where the story is. The phases are just the setting.

---

## Case study: OpenSSL before Heartbleed

In 2014 a bug in OpenSSL exposed memory on roughly half the internet's secure
servers.

The commit history had been telling the story for years: a codebase critical to
everything, maintained by a very small number of people, funded by almost
nobody.

Bus factor is visible in the log, if you read it.

---

## The limits of dating

Commit timestamps lie:

- Clock skew on the author's machine
- `git rebase` rewrites author dates
- Squash merges collapse weeks into one commit
- Bulk imports date an entire codebase to one afternoon

Date the project, then look for reasons to distrust your own dating.
