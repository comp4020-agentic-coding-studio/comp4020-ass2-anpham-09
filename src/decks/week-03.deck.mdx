# Reading the inscriptions

SLOP2374 · Week 3

---

## A commit message is a genre

It has conventions, an audience, and a house style.

Choosing a convention is a statement about who you expect to read the history.
So is choosing not to have one.

---

## Four kinds

- **Descriptive:** what changed and why
- **Terse:** what changed
- **Opaque:** `wip`, `stuff`, `.`
- **Conventional:** `feat:`, `fix:`, `chore:`

Count the proportions on your dig site. The ratio is the finding.

---

## What terse means

Four readings of the same short message:

- Low effort
- High velocity
- Working alone, with no audience in mind
- Exhaustion

The commit message alone will not tell you which. The dates around it might.

---

## Case study: the Linux kernel

Torvalds's merge commits are editorial: they summarise other people's work, in
his voice, for a reader who was not in the discussion.

Very few projects write history for a future reader. The ones that do are
unmistakable.

---

## Provenance in the trailers

`Signed-off-by:` is the Developer Certificate of Origin — a legal assertion
about where code came from.

`Co-authored-by:`, `Reviewed-by:`, `Reported-by:` are a social graph nobody
drew on purpose.

Archaeological metadata, attached to every artefact.

---

## What they cannot tell you

The conversation happened before the commit.

The argument, the rejected design, the thing someone chose not to say in
writing — none of it is in the log.

This is why week 7 goes looking for people.
