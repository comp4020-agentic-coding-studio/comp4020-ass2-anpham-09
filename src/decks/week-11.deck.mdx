# Minimum intervention

SLOP8374 · Week 11

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> A conservator stabilises a painting.
>
> They do not repaint it.

---

## The conservation principle

Change only what is necessary to make it work.

Document every change, in order, each with a justification.

Stop as soon as it runs.

---

## What "minimum" means in practice

- Updating a dependency, not rewriting the code that calls it
- A shim, not a migration
- Restoring compatibility, not adding features
- Leaving the formatting alone, however much it hurts

---

## Three levels of intervention

From least to most intrusive:

| Level | Method | Original code |
|-------|--------|---------------|
| **Shim** | A compatibility layer around it | Untouched |
| **Migration** | Update the calls to a living API | Changed mechanically |
| **Rewrite** | Replace the implementation | Gone |

The conservator's preference is always the least intrusive option that
achieves stability.

---

## The restoration log

A complete, ordered list of every change and why you made it.

The log is as important as the code. Without it, your restoration is
indistinguishable from a rewrite — and unreviewable.

---

## Case study: HTMX

HTMX revives ideas that were standard in 2005: HTML over the wire, progressive
enhancement, the server owning state.

Restoration or reinvention? It kept the model and replaced the implementation —
which is exactly the Ship of Theseus question from last week, shipped as a
library.

---

## What you deliberately do not change

The temptation is constant: refactor, modernise, improve.

Every improvement is a departure from the original. That may be correct — but
it must be a decision you made, written down, not a habit you did not notice.

---

## When restoration is wrong

Some projects should stay dead:

- Superseded, with a maintained successor
- Insecure by design
- Archived with an explicit request
- Finished

Choosing not to restore is a legitimate finding.
