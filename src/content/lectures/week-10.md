## Learning outcomes

By the end of this week you should be able to:

1. distinguish what a licence permits from what an author intended, and argue about the gap
2. locate the revival of abandoned work within established conservation-ethics debates
3. formulate and defend a position on whether a specific project should be revived

## Outline

- **What a licence permits vs what an author intended:** the archived repo as a statement
- **The spectrum of abandonment:** "looking for maintainers" to silence to "this project is archived and read-only" to deletion
- **Forking ethics:** when is a fork a continuation and when is it appropriation?
- **Case study: io.js / Node.js** — a fork born of governance frustration that merged back. Was it a hostile fork or a rescue?
- **The Ship of Theseus:** if you replace every dependency, update every API call, and rewrite the tests, is it the same project?
- **Naming a fork:** the ethics of keeping the original name vs choosing a new one
- **Community ownership after abandonment:** who speaks for a project when nobody maintains it?

## Required reading

- Muñoz Viñas, S. (2005) *Contemporary Theory of Conservation*. Elsevier. Chapters 4–6.
- Coleman, E.G. (2013) *Coding Freedom: The Ethics and Aesthetics of Hacking*. Princeton University Press. Chapters 1 and 6.

## Further reading

- Ruskin, J. (1849) 'The Lamp of Memory', in *The Seven Lamps of Architecture*.
- Kelty, C.M. (2008) *Two Bits: The Cultural Significance of Free Software*. Duke University Press.

## Seminar questions

1. Ruskin held that restoration is 'a destruction accompanied with false description'; Viollet-le-Duc held that a building should be returned to a completeness it may never have had. Which position does a software fork take?
2. Muñoz Viñas argues conservation serves present communities rather than objects. Who is the community of an abandoned library with two hundred downloads a week?
3. Is there any case where a permissive licence should not be exercised? Name one and defend it.
