## Learning outcomes

By the end of this week you should be able to:

1. distinguish abandonment from dormancy and from completion, and state what evidence separates them
2. situate software decay within the existing literature on software ageing and evolution
3. select and justify a dig site adequate to twelve weeks of sustained enquiry

## Outline

- What is software archaeology? The term, the literature, the practice
- **The scale of abandonment:** how many npm packages, PyPI packages, and GitHub repos have no human commit in the last two years
- **Dead vs dormant vs done:** three kinds of silence
- **Case study: Google Reader** — a product killed by strategy, mourned by users, archived by nobody
- **Choosing a dig site:** size, licence, history depth, diagnosability
- **The semester plan:** one codebase, twelve methods, one exhibit

## Required reading

- Parnas, D.L. (1994) 'Software Aging', *Proceedings of the 16th International Conference on Software Engineering* (ICSE-16), pp. 279–287.
- Eghbal, N. (2020) *Working in Public: The Making and Maintenance of Open Source Software*. Stripe Press. Chapters 1–2.

## Further reading

- Lehman, M.M. (1980) 'Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution', *Proceedings of the IEEE* 68(9), pp. 1060–1076.
- Kirschenbaum, M.G. (2008) *Mechanisms: New Media and the Forensic Imagination*. MIT Press. Introduction and Chapter 1 on forensic and formal materiality.

## Seminar questions

1. Parnas argues software ages for two distinct reasons. Which of the two is visible in a commit history, and which is not?
2. Is 'dead' a property of a codebase, of its community, or of its users' expectations? Does your answer change which projects count as dead?
3. Eghbal reframes open source as a maintenance problem rather than a production problem. What does that reframing make visible that the 'bazaar' account does not?
